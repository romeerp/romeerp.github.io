Searching for a better proxy than KL for quantized models

KL doesn't track quantized model performance very accurately. Interestingly, most lost accuracy comes from one kind of mistake that KL can't see.

Quantizing a language model stores its weights in fewer bits. The model gets cheaper to run and slightly worse. To judge how much worse without running a full benchmark, people usually measure the KL divergence between the quantized model and the original. KL is also the standard way to win the accuracy back: quantization-aware distillation trains the quantized model to make that KL small.

In a post on making quantized models more accurate, Applied Compute showed that KL is an unreliable guide to accuracy. One distillation recipe cut KL by 41% and recovered none of the lost task reward; another cut KL by only 10% and recovered all of it. They posed an open question: what other metrics might be better proxies for preserving evaluation accuracy following quantization?

I became interested in figuring out what exactly an accurate proxy would look like. Most of the accuracy a quantized model loses turns out to come from one kind of mistake. At a step where the original model is nearly certain about the next token, the quantized model confidently picks one of the original's close runner-up options instead, a plausible-looking but wrong token. KL gives these moments very little weight. Measuring these swaps directly gives a proxy that orders quantized models by accuracy correctly 93% of the time, against 66% for KL.

Setup#

Most of what follows uses Qwen3-8B on 1,819 math problems (GSM8K and MATH-500). Accuracy is the fraction answered correctly, averaged over several sampled solutions per problem.

I made quantized versions two ways:

  • Post-training quantization: round every weight to a 3- or 4-bit format (MXFP4, NVFP4, INT4, INT3, and variants that quantize only some layers) and stop.
  • Quantization-aware distillation: start from a rounded model and keep training it. The forward pass uses the rounded weights, and gradient descent updates the underlying full-precision weights so the quantized model's predictions match the original's.

KL compares the two models' next-token distributions at a position, p for the original and q for the quantized model:

KL and accuracy disagree#

KL gets the broad trend right, but it can be misleading:

  • Same KL, very different accuracy. The two circled models both have KL around 0.035. The untrained one loses 2.2 points of accuracy. The one that went through distillation loses 7.4.
  • Less KL, less accuracy. Halfway through one distillation run, the model has half the KL of the model it started from (0.018 against 0.034), but it is less accurate (−3.1 points against −2.2).

Why KL doesn't fully measure accuracy#

A model writes its answer one token at a time, picking each token from a probability distribution. Most choices don't affect the answer: "Let's" or "We" lead to the same place. A few do. Suppose the model has written:

The capital of Australia is

The original model gives "Canberra" a probability of about 0.9997 here, and its runner-up, "Sydney", about 0.0003. If a quantized model writes "Sydney", the answer is wrong. Accuracy depends on a small number of decisive choices like this one: places where the original is nearly sure, and the alternative it considered is wrong.

KL measures something different. At every position, it adds up how much the probability of each token changed, weighting each change by how likely the original thought that token was. That weighting has two consequences.

1. Most of KL comes from positions that don't matter. Sort positions by how confident the original is in its top token. The 20% where it's least confident produce 85% of the KL. Those are mostly interchangeable phrasings, where the original itself was unsure. The decisive positions are the opposite: places where it's nearly certain.

2. At the decisive positions, the dangerous token is nearly invisible. Suppose quantization shifts the probabilities like this:

"Canberra" "Sydney"
original 0.9997 0.0003
quantized 0.996 0.004

"Sydney" became 13 times more likely, a real step toward a wrong answer. But its change is weighted by the original's probability of it, 0.0003, so it barely registers: this position's KL is 0.003, less than an average position contributes.

So KL puts most of its weight where the original is unsure, and very little on rare tokens at positions where it's confident. Those rare tokens are where errors that change answers would show up, so that's where I looked next.

Looking at the rare tokens#

Quantization does change these tokens. At positions where the original gives its top token more than 90%, the quantized models put 3.5 to 9 times more probability on tokens the original gives under 1 in 1,000. Rounding the weights takes a little probability from the top token and spreads it into the tail, and the rarest tokens gain the most.

Most of that spread doesn't matter on its own: even at 0.004, "Sydney" gets written well under 1% of the time. What matters is what the quantized model commits to, which can be measured by cooling its distribution:

Cooling. Divide the quantized model's logits by T = 0.1 before the softmax. That raises every probability to the 10th power before renormalizing, so any gap between options becomes huge.

quantized model after cooling
"Canberra" 0.996, "Sydney" 0.004 (drifting) "Canberra" 1.000, "Sydney" 0.000
"Canberra" 0.5, "Sydney" 0.5 (a tie) "Canberra" 0.5, "Sydney" 0.5
"Canberra" 0.29, "Sydney" 0.70 (committed to the wrong answer) "Canberra" 0.0001, "Sydney" 0.9999

After cooling, each position's distribution is essentially the quantized model's favorite token, with credit split only when two options are nearly tied.

Which rare tokens cost accuracy#

When a quantized model commits to a token the original considers unlikely, it's usually one of three kinds:

  • Formatting quirks: a stray symbol or space where the original wouldn't put one.
  • Glitches: with MXFP4, an internal activation occasionally blows up at one position, the next-token distribution goes nearly flat, and the model writes a nonsense token before picking the thread back up.
  • Plausible wrong answers: "Sydney" for "Canberra".

To measure which of these cost accuracy, I collected about 1,500 swaps: places in a quantized model's own solutions where it wrote something other than the original's top choice. From each one, the original model finished the solution twice, once after the quantized model's token and once after its own, always taking the most likely next token. Both versions were graded. The harm of a type of swap is how much more often the original-token version is correct.

I grouped swaps by the substituted token's rank: its position when the original's candidates are sorted from most to least likely (the top choice is rank 1).

The harm depends on what the substitute is, not just on how unlikely it is. A rare token far down the original's list costs almost nothing. A rare token the original ranks 2nd to 5th, a near runner-up, breaks a correct answer 12% of the time.

A far-out token is obviously wrong: a stray symbol or a nonsense word stands out, the intended meaning is still clear from context, and the model carries on. A near runner-up looks right: "Sydney" is a reasonable thing to write, nothing later flags it, and the rest of the answer builds on it.

Distillation fixes the harmless kind and keeps the harmful kind#

Take positions where a quantized model's favorite token is one the original gives under 1 in 1,000:

  • Before distillation, 52–84% of those tokens (depending on the format) rank below 16th in the original's list: formatting quirks and glitches.
  • After standard distillation, 72–83% of the remaining ones rank 2nd to 5th: plausible wrong answers.

Distillation removes the glitches and cleans up the formatting, which lowers KL a lot but changes accuracy very little. The swaps that do cost accuracy carry almost no KL, so they barely get trained away. That's how a distilled model can have the same KL as an untrained one and lose more than three times as much accuracy.

A better proxy: the near-runner-up swap rate#

The proxy measures only the second kind:

Near runner-up. At a given position, a token is a near runner-up if the original gives it under 1% probability and it is still among the original's 16 most likely tokens.

Near-runner-up swap rate. Have the quantized model write one solution per problem. Run both models over those solutions. At each position, cool the quantized model's distribution (T = 0.1) and add up its probability on that position's near runner-ups. Average over all positions.

In plain terms, it is roughly the share of positions where the quantized model's favorite token is one of the original's plausible-but-wrong runner-ups. A committed "Sydney" counts as 1, a drifting one as 0, and a stray symbol as 0 however confident the quantized model is, because it's outside the original's top 16.

It costs about the same as KL, one pass of each model over the quantized model's solutions, and needs no answer key.

To compare it with KL, I use ordering accuracy. Take every pair of quantized models whose accuracies are reliably different: when the problem set is resampled 500 times, the same model comes out ahead at least 95% of the time. Then count the fraction of those pairs the measure puts in the right order. 50% is a coin flip.

I then trained six more distillation checkpoints with three different objectives (forward KL, reverse KL and one-hot cross-entropy), each saved halfway through and at the end:

ordering accuracy KL near-runner-up swap rate
the first 19 models 85% 97%
pairs involving the 6 new checkpoints 66% 93%

On the new pairs where the two measures disagree, the swap rate is right 27 times out of 27. For example, KL rates the worst new checkpoint, which lost 7.4 points, as better than one that lost 1.6.

What this means for training#

A natural next step is to train against these swaps directly. Two results suggest that isn't straightforward.

Upweighting the swaps made things worse. Giving the training loss 10× extra weight at positions where the quantized model currently makes a near-runner-up swap made accuracy worse (−1.8 points) and the swaps more common. Model-update research has seen the same failure: positive-congruent training, which protects an old model's correct decisions, breaks down when the protected cases are weighted too heavily. All the swaps are fixed through the same weights, so concentrating the correction on a handful of positions tends to patch those positions while disturbing the rest.

A smooth version of the swap rate rewards the wrong behavior. Cooling makes the swap rate a count, and counts don't give useful gradients. The obvious smooth alternative is the quantized model's raw probability on near runner-ups, without cooling. But it ranks models backwards, getting only 12% of the new pairs right. The damaged checkpoints have less of it, because they became more confident everywhere: that drains probability from runner-ups at the thousands of positions where they're right, while they commit harder at the few where they're wrong. A loss built from it would reward exactly that failure.

Classifiers have the same split: they're evaluated on accuracy but trained on cross-entropy, because accuracy is a count with no useful gradient. For quantized models, KL has been used for both jobs. These results suggest separating them: the swap rate as the measure of quality, and a training loss chosen because it improves that measure. Finding that loss is still open.

Conclusion#

KL doesn't accurately track the performance of quantized models because it measures divergence everywhere, weighted by how likely the original thinks each token is, while the divergence that costs accuracy is concentrated in one place: rare tokens at positions where the original is nearly certain. Within those, one kind of mistake accounts for most of the damage: confidently writing one of the original's close runner-ups, a plausible-looking but wrong token. Measuring those swaps gives a proxy that orders quantized models by accuracy much more reliably than KL, at about the same cost and without an answer key.
