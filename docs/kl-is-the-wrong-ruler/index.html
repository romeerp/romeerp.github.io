KL is the wrong ruler for quantized models

KL measures how different two models' predictions are. Accuracy depends on the mistakes the quantized model actually makes.

Quantizing a language model stores its weights in fewer bits, which makes it cheaper to run and slightly worse at everything. How much worse? Running a full evaluation after every change is slow and expensive, so most people use a faster check: the KL divergence between the quantized model and the original.

It usually works. But Applied Compute noticed that when you train a quantized model to recover accuracy, KL can fall while accuracy doesn't. I ran into the same thing and wanted to understand why, and what to measure instead.

The short version: KL measures how different the two models' predictions are on the original model's text. What actually costs accuracy is the quantized model making mistakes on its own solutions. Those turn out to be quite different things, and a simple count of the second one tracks accuracy better than KL.

Background: a language model as a decision-maker#

It helps to look at a language model the way reinforcement learning looks at an agent.

When a model solves a math problem, it writes one token at a time. At each step it sees everything written so far (the state) and picks the next token (the action) from a probability distribution. A complete solution is a sequence of these choices, and at the end we check the answer: the reward is 1 if it's right and 0 if it's wrong. Accuracy is the average reward.

A quantized model is the same decision-maker with slightly perturbed probabilities. Call the original model p and the quantized one q.

KL divergence measures how different two probability distributions are. At one position, if the original model gives "7" probability 0.99 and the quantized model gives it 0.9, KL is small. If the quantized model gives it 0.001, KL is large. The usual damage score averages this over every position of some text, normally text written by the original model.

To talk about which choices matter, we need two more ideas:

  • Value: the chance that the original model, continuing from a given state, ends with the right answer.
  • Advantage of a token: how much choosing that token changes the value. Writing "Let's" instead of "We" usually changes nothing: advantage near zero. Writing the wrong digit in the middle of a calculation can drop the chance of a correct answer from 90% to 0%: a large negative advantage.

A classic result from reinforcement learning, the performance difference lemma, says exactly how much accuracy one model loses relative to another:

acc(q)−acc(p)=Esolutions written by q[∑tAp(statet,tokent)]\text{acc}(q) - \text{acc}(p) = \mathbb{E}_{\text{solutions written by } q}\Big[\sum_t A_p(\text{state}_t, \text{token}_t)\Big]acc(q)−acc(p)=Esolutions written by q​[t∑​Ap​(statet​,tokent​)]

In words: follow the quantized model as it writes its own solutions. At each token, ask how much that token changed the original model's chance of getting the answer right. Add those up, and on average you get exactly the change in accuracy. Tokens that hurt count as negative, so a quantized model that keeps picking harmful tokens ends up with a large negative total.

Two things about this formula matter for everything that follows. It's computed on the quantized model's own solutions, not the original model's. And it only counts tokens that change the outcome. KL does neither.

The puzzle#

I quantized Qwen3-8B in many ways and measured accuracy on 1,819 grade-school and competition math problems. Some checkpoints used plain post-training quantization (PTQ): round the weights and stop. Others used quantization-aware distillation (QAD): after rounding, train the quantized model to imitate the original, usually by minimizing KL. Altogether I made 45 checkpoints.

KL often put them in the wrong order:

  • Same KL, different accuracy. One PTQ format and one QAD checkpoint both had KL ≈ 0.013. The PTQ model lost 0.5 points of accuracy; the QAD model lost 1.9.
  • Different KL, same accuracy. Two QAD checkpoints trained with different losses lost the same accuracy, but one had 3× the KL of the other.
  • KL falls while accuracy falls. Halfway through one QAD run, the model had half the KL of the model it started from but lower accuracy.

Within one kind of checkpoint, say a set of PTQ formats, KL does reasonably well. It breaks when you compare checkpoints made in different ways, which is exactly the comparison you need when deciding between recipes.

The hypothesis: a weighted KL#

The lemma suggests what's wrong. KL treats every position the same, but accuracy only depends on positions where the choice changes the outcome. So my starting hypothesis was that we need a weighted KL: count divergence where tokens matter and ignore it where they don't.

The ideal weight is the advantage itself, but computing it means running many continuations from every position, which costs far more than an evaluation. We need a cheap stand-in. So which positions matter?

To find out, I took solutions written by the original model, picked 1,792 positions, and at each one forced each of the model's top four candidate tokens. Then I let it finish 16 times from each and checked whether its accuracy depended on the choice.

About 10% of positions mattered, and they were overwhelmingly positions where the model was nearly certain: the next number in a calculation, the term being substituted. Where the model hesitates, between "Let's" and "We" or where to break a line, the options are interchangeable. The model's uncertainty points almost exactly away from the decisions that matter.

KL looks the other way: 85% of it comes from the 20% of positions where the model is least sure. Those are mostly wording choices. That explains how distillation can lower KL without helping accuracy: it's easiest to reduce KL on exactly the positions that don't matter.

So the natural weighted KL is simple: only count KL at positions where the original model is confident, say where its top token has probability above 0.99.

Why the obvious fix isn't enough#

At first this looked like the answer. On distillation checkpoints, including 18 I trained after settling on it, confident-position KL ordered pairs of checkpoints by accuracy correctly 97% of the time, against 92% for plain KL.

But a proxy chosen on one set of models can fail on the next, so I built a test designed to break it. I generated 43 new quantization variants, using different number formats and quantizing different parts of the network. Without measuring any accuracy, I picked the 14 on which plain KL and confident-position KL disagreed most. Then I measured accuracy to see which was right.

Confident-position KL did worse than a coin flip. Where the two measures disagreed, it was right on only 5 of 40 pairs.

Looking at the failures showed why. In one variant, 99% of its confident-position KL came from 1% of positions, a few formatting spots like the decimal point after 0. There, the quantized model gave the original model's token almost no probability, so each such spot added around 10 units of KL, while a typical position adds almost nothing. But the model rarely acted on them: when it wrote its own solutions, it didn't commit to a wrong token there, and its accuracy barely moved.

Weighting fixed where KL looks, but not what it measures. Two problems remained, both visible in the lemma:

  1. KL's penalty per token is unbounded, but one wrong token can at most fail one problem. A few extreme positions can swamp everything else.
  2. KL measures disagreement in probabilities, but what costs accuracy is a token the quantized model actually writes, on its own path.

Counting committed errors#

The lemma says to follow the quantized model through its own solutions and add up the damage from the tokens it picks. We can't compute the damage exactly, but we can recognize the tokens that cause most of it. This gives a rule:

  1. Have the quantized model write one solution per problem.
  2. Run the original model over each solution to get its probability for every token.
  3. Count the tokens where the quantized model was confident (probability above 0.3) and the original model rules the token out (probability below 0.1%).
  4. Divide by the number of solutions. I call this the confident-error rate.

Each piece maps onto the lemma:

  • The quantized model's own solutions are the states the lemma averages over.
  • "The original model rules it out" picks out tokens with large negative advantage. At positions where the original model is confident, the alternatives are almost always worse.
  • Counting gives each error a bounded cost, like a failed problem. Versions that add up log-probability ratios did no better than KL.
  • "The quantized model was confident" separates real mistakes from noise. Low-confidence oddities are mostly harmless wording. Confident ones are mostly substantive: wrong numbers, wrong operations. They also persist. Resampling from the same point brings a confident wrong token back about three times in four, and a low-confidence one almost never.

To check that these tokens really cause failures, I took solutions containing one and resampled the rest of the solution twice: once keeping the token, once swapping in the original model's choice. On problems the original model always solves, for a checkpoint from the middle of a distillation run, keeping the token made failure about seven times more likely (25% against 3.5%).

Does it work?#

I fixed this rule before training the checkpoints it was later tested on, so those are a fair test. On the 32 checkpoints made afterwards, it orders pairs by accuracy correctly 94% of the time, against 90% for KL. Where the two disagree, it's right on 26 of 40 pairs. On the test built to break confident-position KL, it ties plain KL at 88%. It's the only measure I tried that never did worse than plain KL on any group of checkpoints.

It's also not fragile. Moving the quantized-model threshold anywhere from 0.1 to 0.5, or the original-model threshold anywhere from 1% to 0.01%, still beats KL on the checkpoints outside that test.

What it costs, and when to use it#

On one H100, for about 1,800 problems, the confident-error rate takes roughly three and a half minutes: about two to have the quantized model write one solution per problem, and about one and a half to run the models over those solutions. Plain KL takes about one and a half minutes. Neither needs an answer key, a grader, or anything specific to math.

That raises an obvious question. If the quantized model is already writing one solution per problem, and you do have an answer key, why not just grade those solutions?

I checked, scoring every measure against accuracy from separate samples so that no measure gets graded against itself. That shifts the numbers above by one to three points:

mean KL confident-error rate one graded solution per problem
distillation checkpoints made later (18) 93% 96% 92%
quantization test built to split the measures (14) 88% 91% 96%
all checkpoints made later (32) 91% 95% 94%
all 45 checkpoints 85% 92% 97%

Grading one solution is about as good on the later checkpoints, and better overall. So if you can grade, grade.

The confident-error rate is for when you can't: open-ended tasks with no reference answers, prompts you haven't labeled, or a cheap check while a distillation run is training. It also tells you where the quantized model goes wrong, not just how often. In all of those settings, it's a better default than KL.

What this means for distillation#

QAD usually minimizes KL, and most KL sits on wording choices, so the loss can keep improving while the mistakes that matter barely move. In my runs this was clearest with aggressive training. At a high learning rate, accuracy dipped below the untrained starting point partway through training, even though KL kept falling. A gentler learning rate avoided the dip and recovered about three times as much accuracy by the end.

The confident-error rate isn't perfect here either: it misses part of that mid-training dip. I also tried training directly on confident-position KL, and it didn't beat ordinary KL training. For now the new measure is a way to evaluate, not a better loss.

Takeaways#

  • KL answers a different question from the one you care about. It asks how different the predictions are on the original model's text. Accuracy depends on which mistakes the quantized model makes on its own.
  • Weighting KL toward important positions helps, but isn't enough. A few extreme positions dominate it, and probability gaps aren't mistakes.
  • Count the mistakes directly: confident tokens in the quantized model's own output that the original model rules out. It beats KL at a similar cost and needs no answer key. If you do have one, grading a solution per problem works at least as well.

All of this comes from one model, on math, with weight-only quantization. The natural next step is to check that the rule carries over to other models and tasks.
